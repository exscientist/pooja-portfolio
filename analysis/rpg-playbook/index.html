[reverse-engineering Rapid Product Growth]

screen→rewrite the offer→fix the sales call→cold email→Meta ads→booked calls→scale.
What a growth agency would do for Rex Black, week by week.

This page walks through what Rapid Product Growth (“RPG”) would do for Rex Black after a contract is signed, from the first week to month three and beyond. It is written for someone who has read nothing else. It is reconstructed from RPG’s own method videos, the 7-minute sales video on their booking page, a 45-minute podcast interview in which Jean-Michel Moreau describes his process, and the ads RPG is running right now from two clients’ own Facebook pages. Where a number appears, it is his number, and the source is named.

01 · In one page

RPG does not start with ads. It starts by rewriting what Rex Black sells to strangers.

The new thing to sell is a small project with a fixed price, a fixed deadline, a clearly named result, and a money-back clause, aimed at a demand trigger that already exists in the market. Only after that offer exists do they touch any marketing channel.

Then it works from the back of the funnel to the front. First they fix how Rex Black sells: record every sales call, pull out the objections, retrain the team to sell to cold buyers. Second they tune cold email outreach, aiming for two to five percent of people replying, and use podcast invitations as an indirect way in. Only then do they run Facebook and Instagram ads, and those ads sell a phone call rather than the service. The ads run from Rex Black’s own Facebook page, five or six versions at a time, paid for with $5,000 to $10,000 a month of Rex Black’s money.

One number governs everything: the cost of getting one qualified prospect to book a call and actually show up. The benchmarks he uses are $250 to $500 per booked call from Meta ads for a normal B2B company, an 80 percent show rate when things are working, and a 20 percent close rate on calls that were booked for a specific offer. Put together, that means $1,500 to $6,000 to win a customer. He expects the first calls within 30 days, cash within 60 to 90 days, and the whole funnel “unlocked” within one to three months. If a company’s numbers cannot beat those, he says so on the first call and does not take the deal.

Alongside the system, he sits in the client’s sales calls, rewrites how discovery and the presentation are run, and proposes the bigger engagement inside the first two weeks. In his words, that happens “at the point of maximum deprivation, before the problem is solved.” Each step of the machine is documented on his own channel.

The four checks he applies to every applicant

He uses the call to test whether the company fits. If any check fails, he declines.

Passes

Are there enough buyers?

“At least 10,000 possible customers… if there’s 50 or 100,000, it’s way easier.” Below that, he says ads are the wrong tool.

Rex Black sells to D2C brands on Shopify, Salesforce Commerce Cloud and Magento, and to agencies that need white-label developers. That is tens of thousands of companies in North America.

Passes

Is each customer worth enough?

A yearly contract value of at least $25,000 (sales video); “30k would be nice” (Noob School). He turns away cheap software and low-ticket offers.

Rex Black’s custom builds and QA retainers sit well above this line.

Open

Is there spare cash?

“$5–10K per month in ad spend” for three to four months before you learn anything. “If that feels like a stressful thing to do, you’re not ready.”

This is the CEO’s decision. The ad money comes on top of RPG’s fee.

Read on the call

Is the founder committed?

He rejects founders whose attitude is “everything doesn’t work for me, prove it.” He wants “there’s no reason why this should be impossible.”

He reads the founder for this in the conversation itself, not from a form.

He also talks openly about rejection when he explains the guarantee. In the ad he calls “we copied Harvard” and in his vendor-audit videos, he tells “the three times it didn’t work.” He explains that most engagements begin as fractional work, meaning a part-time paid role auditing what the company already does: “I come in to evaluate all their vendors… very often we come up with a more involved engagement within the first two weeks.” So the realistic expectation from a first proposal is a small paid audit, not the full program.

03 · The engagement, phase by phase

Seven phases over twelve weeks, and they overlap

The sequence below merges four sources that describe the same process at different levels of detail: the four-step “Scaling Flywheel” in his sales video, his 11-step “Growth Systems” video, the 8-step ad flowchart in his video on de-risking paid acquisition, and his answer to the question “once you figure all that out for a client, then what?” on the Noob School podcast. Week 0Diagnostic

Look under the hood, score every channel, name the one blockage

  1. Audit every vendor and channel. He puts everything on one sheet and measures each one the same way: how much it costs to get one qualified prospect to book a call and show up. From an audit he shows on camera: an outbound calling agency charging $10,000 a month produced six bookings and two showed calls in six weeks, which he works out to “almost $7,000 per showed call… cut this team.” The Meta ads in the same company were underfunded (“they should have spent $7,000 already”). LinkedIn ads offering a white paper produced leads at about $180 each that never turned into calls, so his question became “what’s up with the sales team?”
  2. Find the single blockage using five boxes. The blockage is always one of: not enough leads, leads that do not buy, customers who leave, no spare cash, or the CEO having no time. “It has to be one.” The questions he asks to find it: how many qualified sales calls per week, and what was the best week ever; cost per call by channel; what share of first contacts turn into a demo; the close rate on qualified calls (he expects 20 to 30 percent); how much free cash could be thrown at the blockage; and whether the CEO can get three uninterrupted hours.
  3. Work out the ideal customer from the client’s own data. A spreadsheet of every past client: which channel they came from, what kind of company they are, their revenue level, who made the buying decision, what event triggered the purchase, how good a fit they seemed at first, and total money spent. From that he calculates the average lifetime value and the lifetime value of the top 20 percent of clients, then picks one segment to chase. His reasoning: “It’s way more profitable to help winners win than to help strugglers stop struggling.”
  4. Dissect the competition. Three direct competitors and three indirect ones, ten questions each: what they promise, what they claim, what proof they show, what benefits they list, what makes their method unique, fragments of good copy, whether they have a unique selling point, what their winning ads look like (found in the Facebook Ad Library and a tool called VidTao), what customers complain about, and exactly what they offer at what price. His view: “The best research has already been done by all your competitors.”
  5. Fill in his “Rapid Marketing Canvas.” A board that lists the buyer’s pains, their dream outcome, the jobs they need done, the false beliefs they hold, and their immediate small problems. Guesses go on red sticky notes and become green only when real data confirms them. From this he draws a “belief spectrum”: the sequence of beliefs a buyer must move through, from their first wrong assumption to the “one belief” that Rex Black’s way of working is the best solution. For each step he looks for a “black swan,” meaning one piece of evidence so clear that it changes the belief without arguing.
Evidence

“Unless you think you’re perfect at everything, have this crew snoop around. We pretty quickly found areas where it was like, I totally know how to do that, I’ll have it done next week.” That is Steve Benson of Badger Maps, quoted on RPG’s website.

In a video with Steve, Jean-Michel describes the mechanics: the client’s marketing team presents what they are doing, he ranks possible projects by likely impact, he restarts channels that were dead (an automated LinkedIn outreach tool) and makes small adjustments to channels that were alive, which he calls turning them “four degrees to the left,” using specific software such as a $150-a-month tool that checks whether emails land in the inbox.

WhyHe wants real numbers before he changes anything, and he only works on one blockage at a time.
Weeks 1–3Offer

Rework the offer so a stranger can say yes

He treats this step as the whole game, and as the reason previous agencies failed the client. It is done with the CEO in the room.

  1. Ignore what Rex Black “does.” His instruction: “Ignore the company’s overall solution. Look at the outcomes the customer can point at and say ‘that happened.’” Break the complicated service into outcome one, outcome two, outcome three.
  2. Rank the outcomes by what makes a buyer stop scrolling. “New revenue first. Cashflow second. Everything else loses.”
  3. Attach the offer to demand that already exists instead of educating people. His “hot dog stand” rule: if the queue wants hot dogs, sell hot dogs, not artisan hamburgers. His current pick for every client is AI. He says two triggers are live in the market right now: “you bought the AI tools, your business isn’t more valuable,” and “I’d like proven ROI on something around AI.”
  4. Wrap the winning outcome in a stranger-friendly offer. Four ingredients, always: a specific cost, a specific timeframe, a specific outcome, and low risk. Never zero risk, because then “total yahoos take you up on it.” The price sits between $5,000 and $25,000, it is a fixed one-time project, and it is “something whoever you’re dealing with can approve without eight levels of sign-off.” His example wording: “If you got written up about this, we can get you compliant in 30 days or you don’t pay.”
  5. Design the economics before any ad exists. The first project should at least cover the cost of finding the customer, and ideally “pay for themselves plus one customer,” so each sale funds the next one. The profit comes on the first upsell, because in his experience “10% of buyers spend 10x.”
  6. Map five to eight demand triggers. Events that happen before someone starts searching for help: a key account was lost, a new VP wants to make a mark, the board asked questions, a tool stopped scaling. Each trigger becomes an ad angle. His phrase: “Your creative does the targeting,” meaning the ad’s content, not the platform’s settings, decides who responds.
Evidence, live client

2X Solutions is run by Kevin DeMeritt, the founder of Lear Capital. He came to RPG selling a general “AI voice platform,” after a cold-email vendor and a Meta ads agency had both failed. The ads RPG now runs from 2X’s own Facebook page (launched 27 September 2026, eight versions) sell one outcome to one industry: “Mortgage companies are sitting on thousands of leads they’ve already paid for and nobody’s calling them… We’ll Reactivate Your Leads in 30 Days, Or You Don’t Pay. Fill out the form to see if you qualify.” Their cost per booked call fell from $1,500–$2,000 to $500–$750, by RPG’s account.

WhyA stranger will buy a $12,000 fixed project with a guarantee. A stranger will not buy a $200,000 open-ended engagement.
Weeks 2–4Sales

Fix how you sell before buying any traffic

In his words: “Before we do anything about top of funnel, let’s make sure we’re closing everyone we can. This is usually the shortest, quickest thing we can do to add revenue.”

  1. Record every sales call, using a tool like Gong, Fathom or Fireflies, or simply a screen recorder. Review the most important calls of the last month by hand, and run every transcript through an AI prompt (he names the automation tool n8n) to count and group the objections.
  2. Install a “talk track,” a set script for the call that brings out the prospect’s beliefs and worries before price is mentioned.
  3. Retrain the team for cold buyers. He warns: “The sales team will say the leads suck… they’ve only ever sold to their friends.” Cold prospects need a gentler approach: “less pitching on call one, more proof you give a damn, more patience on discovery before anyone talks price.” The warm, referral-based way of selling is kept as it is, and the cold way is added beside it.
  4. If the offer or the price is new, run fifty discovery calls with a strict script, because “if you have 50 discovery calls you can figure anything out.” One client tested 30 different price points in a single week using cheap ads.
  5. Turn each objection into content for retargeting: one belief to change, one piece of evidence, one short video. These are used in the ads phase.
Evidence

From the 2X Solutions ad: “The thing Kevin points to isn’t the ads. It’s that we sat in his sales calls, rebuilt his discovery, and rewrote his presentation.”

From Noob School: “if you take a company, help them get new leads, and stop there, they almost always fail because you need a different sales motion for colder audiences.”

WhyNew leads are wasted if the team can only sell to people who already trust them.
Weeks 2–6Outbound

Turn outreach on, or tune it, because it risks the least cash

  1. The list. Built live with the client in Apollo, a contact database, or bought from a specialist list broker, filtered by industry, country and job title. The target is 10,000 to 15,000 contacts per quarter for email, which he says is “a year of LinkedIn.” He checks pages of the list by eye to catch mistakes.
  2. The plumbing. Six email domains, three to six Google Workspace inboxes on each, the technical deliverability settings done properly, two weeks of warm-up before sending, and a sending tool called Instantly. The target is a reply rate of two percent or better; “my best campaigns are getting 4–5%.”
  3. The approach. Not “ask-based” emails that beg for a meeting. Either give something first, or invite people to something they already wanted: a guest slot on a podcast, a dinner, a contribution to an industry report. The sales discovery happens inside the interview. The cost per booked call is “effectively zero” in money, but about an hour of work per qualified call.
  4. An automated sales assistant. A workflow built in n8n reads replies from Instantly, sorts them by intent, drafts responses into Slack for a human to approve, holds calendar slots, books the meeting through Calendly, and answers common objections from a stored library. A human setter then confirms the appointment and follows up.
  5. The podcast as a pipeline. Two hours a week, four ideal-customer guests a week. Sixty-second clips of the guests are run as ads with no call to action, purely to build a private audience for later retargeting. His claim is that this drops the cost per booked call from $300–$600 to about $50, after roughly five months of nurturing.
Evidence

Wertwandler, a German consultancy, is the outbound proof point: “over 100 calls per month with C-level executives” and “their first six-figure deal with SAP.” There is no Wertwandler advertiser page in Meta’s Ad Library, which fits an outreach-led engagement.

Zight was the podcast-outreach case: “40 qualified calls per month… 120 new conversations,” before Zight “stopped the project for internal reasons.”

WhyOutreach costs time rather than money, so it is the safe channel to fix first.
Weeks 3–8Ads

Meta first, selling a phone call, five or six versions a week

“Meta is one of our first paid channels for B2B clients, not LinkedIn.” Meta means Facebook and Instagram. He uses LinkedIn ads only when a customer is worth over $100,000 and a target list can be built. On LinkedIn, he says, “you bring the intelligence”: you boost the founder’s personal posts through the company ad account and a human adjusts bids every week.

  1. Two campaigns run at once. The first is an awareness campaign of narrow videos about the demand triggers. They are deliberately niche: “we want the rest of the world to scroll past.” Their job is to teach Meta’s system who the buyer is. The second is a lead-generation campaign that advertises the offer with a “book a call” button.
  2. Creative without a studio. Three formats are enough: a three-to-five-minute Loom screen recording of the CEO walking through one trigger, a demo of the product or framework, and an interview of the founder filmed on an iPhone on the desk. For testing, he uses plain, ugly, text-only image ads. His rule: “Simplify your concept; the ads are not about your app.”
  3. Test in a strict order.
    pain→headline→offer→image→body text→Facebook headline
    He runs these as cheap “traffic” campaigns at about $3 per version until each has roughly 400 impressions, so 10 versions cost $30. If needed, 70 or more headlines can be tested in 48 hours. Winners are chosen on click-through rate and cost per click, and he warns that “most of your results will suck.”
  4. Stress-test the winners with more impressions, then choose a path.
    Offer still unproven → chatSend ad clicks straight into chat conversations with no website at all.
    Offer proven → landing pageUse a template landing page, then spend up to two weeks removing friction from it using Mouseflow (a tool that records how visitors scroll) and more headline tests.
  5. Switch to a conversion campaign once the page holds. Judge it after 10 conversions. If the numbers fail, work out whether the page or the ad is the problem, fix that, then scale spending.
  6. The funnel. Ad, then a short qualification form (Typeform or Meta’s own instant form), then a booking page, then a human setter who confirms the call. “When you have ads, you must have a salesperson setting.” He deliberately adds friction so junior people do not get through.
  7. Build the retargeting layer before scaling cold ads. Retargeting means showing more content to people who already engaged. His stated budgets: Facebook $1,500 a month, YouTube $500, LinkedIn $1,000, short vertical videos boosted at $15 a day, direct-message campaigns at $2 a day. The content: a walkthrough of the program, testimonial carousels, one video per objection, the sales video, podcast episodes. Warmed-up prospects are sent straight to a Calendly booking link.
  8. Weekly rhythm. Five or six offers or angles compete against each other every week. Seventy percent of the budget goes to variations of winners and 30 percent to wild new ideas. During testing, the worst video is replaced every day.
Evidence, live client

Susco is a software shop in New Orleans; RPG’s own ad claims it “Booked 100+ calls.” Susco launched 12 ad versions from its own Facebook page on 25 and 26 September 2026, with identical text and different visuals: “Are you still stuck balancing people and profit? Stop believing that simply adding more people is the solution in 2026. Your margins are dying… Increase Your Margins by 35%+.” Underneath is a list, “Proven on: general contractors, home builders, remodelers, roofing, HVAC…”, which mirrors the “Proven on software development shops…” line in RPG’s own ads. The click goes to a Meta instant form. Susco sells custom software; the ad sells a margin outcome to one trade vertical.

WhyWith a good offer, Meta finds the buyers by itself, and a call booked from an ad is with someone who already wants the result.
Weeks 4–12Calls

Book, qualify, make sure they show, close

  1. A sales development rep or setter confirms every booking by text, call and email, and checks the prospect is qualified before the founder’s time is spent. The failure he designs against is the one clients complain about: “They promised us calls and we got calls, but these are junior people.”
  2. The benchmarks he holds the funnel to: $250 to $500 per booked call for normal B2B, about $500 for upper mid-market companies, and up to $5,000 for Fortune 50 targets; an 80 percent show rate when healthy (his worked example assumes 50 percent as “bad numbers”); a 20 percent close rate on calls booked for a specific offer, versus one to three percent on leads who downloaded a white paper. At his bad numbers, filling one salesperson’s day costs about $8,000 of ads.
  3. Cash timing: “You’ll see qualified calls within 30 days of launching… cash collection is typically 60 to 90 days.” He pushes clients to charge on day one rather than day 60, so the first sale funds the next customer.
  4. Upsell timing: “People buy upsells at the point of maximum deprivation… get their trust before you’ve completed solving the entirety of the problem.” In plain words, offer the bigger project while the small one is still running, not after it is finished.
His arithmetic

$7,500 of ads at $375 a call books 20 calls. At 80 percent show, 16 people turn up. At 20 percent close, that is about three first projects a month, so a customer costs roughly $2,300 to win.

At his “bad numbers” (50 percent show), the same spend yields 10 showed calls and two projects.

WhyThe setter is there so the founder’s time only goes to people who fit the offer.
Month 3+Scale

Use your own numbers, kill the next blockage, then leaders and operations

  1. A financial model from real data. The actual cost per call and cost per customer for each channel replace guesses, and decide how much cash goes where. His warning: “It’s possible to run profitable ads that kill your cash flow.”
  2. Find and attack the next blockage using the real numbers against industry benchmarks, and move resources to it rather than polishing things that already work. In his words, that is the point where “you’ve earned the million.”
  3. Leaders and operations. A simple system for what a leader’s job is, so problems stop flowing up to the CEO, and Toyota-style process work so that “growth doesn’t break your business.”
  4. The client rhythm. Weekly calls. A “raise the floor” rule: never test fewer than two things even in a bad week, rather than trying to beat your best week. The founder submits their own plan for that each week.
  5. Handover. “Then we hand you a system you own.” In practice the ad account, the Facebook page, the forms and the workflows already live in the client’s own accounts (Susco and 2X advertise from their own pages), so the accounts, pages and workflows stay with the client at the end.
Where the system lives

Both live clients advertise from their own Facebook pages, and the forms are Meta instant forms or Typeform on the client’s account. The tooling is the client’s from day one.

WhyHe sells the system as something the client keeps, so it is built inside the client’s accounts.

05 · What the CEO would have to do

Low involvement and low investment are two of his four reasons engagements fail

This is the list a client signs up for, in his own terms.

Time

  • A weekly call with the CEO present. “If they’re not showing up, it’s not going to work.”
  • Three uninterrupted hours a week on the current blockage.
  • Sales-team retraining sessions, with Jean-Michel listening to the calls.
  • Two hours a week of podcast interviews, or delegate the hosting to a business-development manager.

Money

  • $5,000 to $10,000 a month of ad spend for at least a quarter, on top of the fee.
  • Tools: Apollo, Instantly plus six domains and inboxes, GoHighLevel or Typeform, Mouseflow, a call recorder, an inbox-placement checker, n8n.
  • A retargeting floor of about $3,000 a month across Facebook, YouTube and LinkedIn if he installs the full infrastructure.

On camera

  • Loom videos, three to five minutes each, one demand trigger per video.
  • An iPhone interview about the triggers.
  • Two short casual videos a week, “walking around.”
  • A short video inside proposals, for the finance chief who was never on the call.

Data and access

  • Every past client, with revenue, who bought and why.
  • Recordings of sales calls.
  • Access to the ad account, the Facebook page and the CRM; “let him snoop around.”
  • Agreement to change pricing: charge earlier, and price the first project at break-even.

07 · Applied to Rex Black

The plan he would draw for Rex Black

This is a projection using his rules and Rex Black’s public positioning: a custom software and QA shop that works with D2C brands and with agencies that need developers. It is what the “roadmap you keep either way” would most likely contain.

The offer he would push Rex Black toward

He colour-codes offers. Yellow is “hard to sell without a relationship, takes an incredible amount of trust.” He used almost that exact phrase about another client’s ad that offered to “place one of our engineers in your project.” Green rides a queue that already exists.

Yellow, in his code“Custom software development”

Rex Black’s current positioning. Sold on trust and referrals. A stranger cannot approve it without a relationship.

Green: D2C brand“Core Web Vitals and checkout speed fixed in 30 days for $12,000, or you don’t pay.”

Tied to revenue before Black Friday. A marketing director can approve it alone. About one in ten of these buyers becomes the $100,000-plus build, by his rule of thumb.

Green: agency“A senior developer on your project within two weeks, fixed monthly price.”

The outcome for an agency is capacity: “a senior developer just left.” Alternative D2C shape: “Klaviyo flows rebuilt before Black Friday, fixed price, three weeks.”

Choosing channels by his own two numbers

Buyers in the market, and value of the first project. Those two numbers pick the channel.

D2C brandsTens of thousands of buyers, and a first-project value in the $10,000 to $50,000 band. That points to a Meta ad funnel that books a call, possibly with a custom audience of Shopify Plus merchants built through a tool like Primer.
Agencies that want white-label developersA smaller list, but worth more per deal. That points to outreach plus podcast invitations (“invite them to something they already wanted”), with LinkedIn ads only if the lifetime value proves to be above $100,000.

  • Direct access to why deals are lost, what clients keep asking for and what projects actually cost to deliver, turned into campaign changes and tested quickly.
  • Every test result, objection and lost-deal insight stays with Rex Black, so each campaign starts from what the last one learned.
  • Experience from inside a D2C brand: the operating realities, priorities and buying considerations of the businesses Rex Black targets.
